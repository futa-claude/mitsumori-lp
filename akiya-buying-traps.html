Six traps in buying a Japanese akiya — and one nobody mentions.

The listing shows you a price and twelve photos. The traps live somewhere else — in the zoning map, the tax office's mailing habits, and the demolition quotes you have not asked for yet. None of the six below are visible in a photograph, and every one of them is checkable from public records before you sign anything. That is the whole point of this page.

The six: ① some parcels lose the right to a new building the day the old one comes down · ② tax notices count as delivered even if you never see them · ③ the property-tax rate is not the same number in every town · ④ demolishing the house can multiply the land tax · ⑤ demolition itself costs seven figures in yen · ⑥ an empty house bills its owner every single year. Plus one more that no listing will ever volunteer.

① The right to build again is not included by default

Urbanisation-control zones, landscape districts, preservation districts, height and design restrictions — all of it is published online, parcel by parcel. If your parcel falls inside one, the practical effect ranges from "you need approval for exterior colours" to "once you demolish, you may not be granted permission to put a new house there at all." The answer for a specific parcel is determined solely by the local building authority — so it has to be checked before you buy, not after you knock something down. A cheap house on a parcel like this is cheap for a reason.

② The tax notice counts as delivered — even to a mailbox you never open

Fixed-asset tax (and, in some zones, city-planning tax) is billed each year to whoever owns the property on January 1. The notice goes to a Japanese address. If that address is the empty house, it is still legally served — instalment dates pass, and arrears with late charges accrue against the property in silence. Nobody chases you internationally; the silence is the trap. The fix costs almost nothing: appoint a tax agent (納税管理人) in Japan — we wrote up exactly how that works.

③ "The rate is 1.4%" is true — except where it isn't

Most English guides quote Japan's standard fixed-asset tax rate of 1.4%. We checked all 26 municipalities in Kyoto Prefecture against their own official pages, and 8 of the 26 charge more: Maizuru is at 1.6% — the highest in the prefecture — and Fukuchiyama, Ayabe, Miyazu, Kameoka, Nantan, Wazuka and Kyotamba are at 1.5%. City-planning tax runs anywhere from zero to 0.3% on top, and nine towns don't levy it at all. But read carefully: Maizuru has no city-planning tax and is still the most expensive rate — "no city-planning tax" does not mean cheap. Two houses with identical price tags in different towns carry different tax bills forever.

④ Demolish the house, and the land tax multiplies

While a house stands on it, small residential land (up to 200 m²) is assessed at one sixth of its normal taxable base. Remove the house and the relief ends: in pure arithmetic that is a 6x jump, and in practice the total usually lands at 3–4x, because the building's own tax bill disappears with the building. Either way, the "free" empty lot is a more expensive thing to own than the shabby house was.

And since 2023 you don't even need a bulldozer to trigger it: a municipality can formally designate a badly kept vacant house as poorly managed, and if its recommendation to fix things is ignored, the tax relief is withdrawn while the house is still standing. Neglect now has a price on it.

⑤ Demolition costs real money — here is the actual spread

For a 118 m² wooden house, from three independent sources:

  • Private-market quotes (73 quotes, Kyoto Prefecture): ¥60,000–73,000 per tsubo → ¥2.1M–2.6M for the whole house.
  • Government-enforced demolitions (national records, 6 wooden cases): ¥1.6M–8.9M scaled to the same floor area — these include surveys and run high.
  • Public bulk-demolition tenders we track: median ¥1.07M per house — a bulk contract price, not what a single owner is offered.

So the realistic private figure for a mid-size wooden house is a couple of million yen — often more than the purchase price of the akiya itself. If your exit plan is "worst case, I'll just tear it down," that plan has a seven-figure line item in it, followed immediately by trap ④.

⑥ The meter runs every year nobody lives there

From our itemised research into what an empty house in rural Kyoto actually costs to hold: a bare-minimum setup (a relative as tax agent, utilities shut off, minimal insurance) can run around ¥42,000 a year. A realistic standard setup — a monthly check on the house, mail forwarded, utilities kept on, insurance, a paid tax agent — comes to roughly ¥260,000 a year, and a heavier configuration with a septic tank, a rural neighbourhood association and fuller management reaches ¥400,000–500,000. None of this appears anywhere near the listing price, and all of it repeats annually, whether you visit or not.

+1 The trap nobody mentions: what happened in the house

Japan has national guidance (issued by the transport-and-housing ministry, MLIT, in 2021) on when agents must disclose a death at a property. The short version: a natural death, or an ordinary accidental death in daily life, generally does not have to be volunteered. But the same guidance says that if the buyer asks, the agent needs to tell. So ask — in writing, before the offer:

この物件で人の死に関わる事案はありましたか。書面でお答えいただけますか。
("Has there been any incident involving a death at this property? Could you answer in writing?")

You can show that on your phone. An agent who answers in writing is an agent you can work with.

The pattern worth remembering

Not one of these six traps is about the building being old. They are about rules and running costs that sit outside the photos — and every single one can be checked from records before money moves: the zoning map, the town's tax page, a demolition quote, a maintenance budget, one written question. The buyers who get burned are not the ones who bought old houses. They are the ones who bought before checking.
