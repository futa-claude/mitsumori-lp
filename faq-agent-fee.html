WHAT CHEAP JAPANESE HOUSES ACTUALLY COST

The agent's fee on a cheap house can be bigger than you think.

Short answer: since July 2024, on a property priced up to ¥8,000,000 the agent may charge a flat fee of up to ¥330,000 (tax included) - and it's legal. On a ¥2,000,000 house that is about 16.5% of the price.
¥330,000
maximum brokerage fee on a house priced up to ¥8M · since July 1, 2024

How the rule works

Japan's brokerage fees are capped by a government notice. The normal formula is roughly 5.5% on the first ¥2M, 4.4% on the next ¥2M, and 3.3% above that - so a ¥2,000,000 house would normally cap out around ¥110,000.

But cheap vacant houses were not worth an agent's time at those rates, so agents simply refused them. The fix, in force since July 1, 2024: for properties priced up to ¥8,000,000 (the "low-priced vacant house" rule), the agent may charge up to ¥300,000 plus consumption tax - ¥330,000 flat - to either the seller or the buyer.

One condition protects you: the agent has to explain this fee and get your agreement up front, when the brokerage contract is made - not at settlement.

What to actually do

  • Ask for the fee in writing before the viewing. In Japanese: 「手数料を書面でいただけますか」- you can show that on your phone.
  • Do the arithmetic on the total, not the sticker price. A "$13,000 house" with a ¥330,000 fee is a different purchase.
  • If the fee was never explained to you before you signed, that is worth questioning - the prior explanation and agreement is required for the higher cap.

The honest other side

The rule exists for a reason: paperwork on a ¥1.5M farmhouse is the same work as on a ¥40M apartment. A good local agent earning ¥330,000 for shepherding a difficult rural transaction is not ripping you off - the problem is only when you find out at the end. Know the number before you fall in love with the house.
