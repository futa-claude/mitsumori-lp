Internet for a rural akiya in Kyoto: what actually works.

A real question from a foreign owner in Kyotamba (京丹波町, rural Kyoto Prefecture, also romanized Kyotanba): "Can't get fiber internet without a local phone number. Got Starlink to watch the cameras we set up, but the latency makes viewing a pain. Any alternative?"

Short answer: in rural Kyoto there are usually three routes, and the best one is the one nobody tells foreign buyers about — the local cable-TV company. In Kyotamba that's ZTV: service in the town since September 2021, an office in the town itself, and fiber speed tests from Kyotamba this August showing ~280 Mbps down. The national fiber map does not always reach the hamlets. The local cable network often does.

Route 1: the local cable company (the one nobody mentions)

Rural Japanese municipalities often solved their own broadband problem years ago — through cable-TV networks, sometimes town-built, now run by regional operators. These networks were laid to reach every hamlet with a TV signal, which means they often physically reach houses that the national fiber footprint skips.

  • Kyotamba: covered by ZTV, a regional cable operator. Their official service-area list names Kyotamba, their corporate history records the town launch in September 2021, and they keep a staffed office in the town (weekdays only). Independent user speed tests posted from Kyotamba in August 2026 show roughly 280 Mbps down on their fiber — the network is real and in daily use.
  • Yosano (north Kyoto): runs its own municipal cable service (与謝野町有線テレビ), listed in the national regulator's registry of cable operators.
  • Your town: search "〔town name〕 ケーブルテレビ インターネット" or ask at the town hall — the same counter that runs the akiya bank usually knows exactly which network reaches which hamlet.

What we could not verify: whether ZTV (or any given local operator) requires a Japanese phone number to sign up. These are local companies with human counters, so the practical move is to have whoever checks on your house — agent, property manager, neighbor — call the local office and ask. That one call settles it.

Route 2: NTT-resale fiber with English support

Several companies resell NTT's fiber network with English-language sign-up. Buyers in the r/Akiya community report completing the whole process without a Japanese phone number — the recurring points from their firsthand accounts:

  • Sign-up can be done remotely and in English.
  • Someone must be at the house on installation day (owners report asking their agent or property manager).
  • It only works where NTT's fiber physically reaches — and in rural Kyoto, parts of a town can be outside that footprint even when the town center is inside. Check your exact address, not the town name.

Route 3: Starlink

Works anywhere with open sky and needs no Japanese paperwork at all — which is why vacation-home owners reach for it first. The trade-offs owners actually report: hardware plus a monthly fee that runs higher than local fiber, and frustration when using it to monitor security cameras remotely. If the house is reachable by cable or fiber, wired service tends to be both cheaper and calmer. If it isn't, Starlink is the honest fallback.

The pattern worth remembering

The "no Japanese phone number" wall is real, but it is a wall of the national providers' sign-up forms — not of the wires in the ground. The more rural the house, the more likely the answer is a local operator with a human counter a few kilometers away. That is the opposite of how it works in most countries, and it is why advice written for Tokyo apartments fails for a farmhouse in Kyotamba.
