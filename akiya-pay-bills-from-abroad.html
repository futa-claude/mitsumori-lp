How to pay property tax and bills on a Japanese akiya from abroad.

You bought the house. You flew home. Then April comes, and somewhere in rural Kyoto a city office prints a property-tax notice — on paper, in Japanese, addressed to the house you are not in. This page is about what happens next, because Japan's billing system quietly assumes every owner lives in Japan.

Short answer: three moving parts. ① A tax agent (納税管理人) in Japan to receive and pay your property tax — this one is a formal appointment, not a favor. ② A mailbox answer — Japan Post states plainly that it does not forward mail overseas, so paper sent to the house needs somewhere to land. ③ A way to move money into Japanese hands cheaply. Solve those three and everything else is detail.

① Property tax: appoint a tax agent (納税管理人)

Fixed-asset tax (固定資産税) is billed once a year, usually payable in instalments — the schedule is printed on the notice. The notice goes to a Japanese address, and payment is designed for Japanese rails: bank counters, convenience stores, direct debit.

A non-resident owner appoints a tax agent — nozei kanrinin — by filing a short form with the municipality that taxes the property. The agent can be anyone in Japan: a friend, a relative, a management firm, in some towns even a willing neighbor. They receive the notices and make the payments on your behalf; you settle up with them.

What happens if you skip it: nothing, at first. The notices pile up in an empty mailbox, the instalment dates pass, and arrears with late charges accumulate against the property — a bill you never saw. It is the single cheapest piece of paperwork in the whole akiya process, and the most skipped.

② The mail is the real trap

Most owners assume they can just forward the mail. They cannot:

  • Japan Post does not forward domestic mail overseas. That is their own FAQ wording, not an interpretation.
  • The domestic forwarding service (転居届) redirects to a Japanese address only — and for one year at a time.

So the practical setups are: forward domestically to your tax agent or property manager (renewing yearly), or accept that the house's mailbox is the terminus and have whoever checks the house photograph what arrives. Either way, someone in Japan is part of the system — there is no pure-remote configuration for paper.

③ Utilities: smaller bills, same problem

Electricity and water for a vacation home are usually kept on a minimum basic-rate contract so the house stays usable. Most utilities offer direct debit from a Japanese bank account or credit-card billing; whether a foreign card is accepted varies by company, and we have not verified any specific provider — ask yours directly. Owners without a Japanese account commonly route utilities through the same person who handles the tax: one point of contact, one settlement.

Moving the money

However you structure it, at some point yen has to reach Japan — the tax agent you reimburse, the manager's invoice, the utility account. Traditional bank wires work but carry fixed fees and exchange margins that sting on small recurring amounts; specialist transfer services (Wise and similar) are what overseas owners in the r/Akiya community most often report using for exactly these small, repeated payments. Compare the total cost — fee plus exchange-rate margin — not just the headline fee.

The pattern worth remembering

Buying the house is one day of paperwork. Paying for it is every year, forever. The whole from-abroad problem reduces to one sentence: appoint someone in Japan to receive paper and pay small bills, and set up a cheap way to send them money. Owners who solve those two things early report the rest of remote ownership is boring — which is exactly what you want it to be.
